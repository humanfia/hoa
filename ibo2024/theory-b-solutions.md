## Task 1 — Dichotomous keys: path length, identification capacity, and phylogeny

**Official answer: A — False; B — True; C — True; D — False.**

### Reasoning

**A — False.** Morphological complexity does not determine how many steps an organism takes through this key. Because the tree is complete and has no terminal branches before level 10, every identification follows a path from the level-1 root to a terminal node at level 10. Such a path contains exactly nine true-or-false decisions, so an organism that can be identified by this key will not require more than nine steps merely because its morphology is more complex than that of X.

**B — True.** Each decision has two possible outcomes, so the number of nodes doubles at every new level. With the root at level 1, the terminal level contains $2^{10-1}=2^9=512$ nodes. If every terminal node identifies a different species, the key can therefore distinguish at most 512 species. There is an arithmetic error in the official explanation: it prints $2^0+2^1+\cdots+2^9=512$, but that sum is 1023 and counts all nodes in the tree. The stated answer remains correct because only the $2^9$ terminal nodes represent final identifications.

**C — True.** From a particular node at level 3, there are seven remaining branchings before level 10, giving $2^{10-3}=2^7=128$ possible terminal identifications below that node. From a node at level 5, there are five remaining branchings, giving $2^{10-5}=2^5=32$ terminal identifications. Thus the level-3 node has $128/32=4$ times as many possible final outcomes.

**D — False.** A dichotomous identification key is organized around whichever diagnostic characters its designer chooses; it is not necessarily a phylogenetic tree. Similar answers to early morphological questions can result from retained ancestral traits or convergent evolution, and close relatives can differ in a character used early in the key. Consequently, the step at which two identification paths separate cannot be inferred with certainty from how recently the taxa shared a common ancestor. Indeed, in a tree that did directly represent ancestry, closer relatives would normally separate later, not earlier, than more distant relatives.

## Task 2 — Red-deer phylogeny and the interpretation of bootstrap support

**Official answer: A — True; B — False; C — False; D — False.**

### Reasoning

**A — True.** The value 89 is attached to the branch that groups the *Cervus* samples after the lineage of *Dama dama* splits away. Thus, in 89 of the 100 bootstrap trees, the complete *Cervus* group was recovered with *Dama* as its sister group. In the other 11 resampled trees, that branch was not recovered, so *Dama* was not sister to the complete *Cervus* group in the sense used by the question. Strictly speaking, a bootstrap value measures recovery of the clade defined by a branch; here the rooting and displayed taxon arrangement make that clade-support interpretation equivalent to the stated sister-group relationship.

**B — False.** Closely related subspecies can be distinguished only if the chosen sequence has accumulated enough differences among them. Histone proteins perform highly constrained structural roles in chromatin, so their genes are generally strongly conserved and tend to provide relatively few informative substitutions at this shallow evolutionary scale. Mitochondrial cytochrome-*b* DNA evolves more rapidly and therefore usually supplies more variation for separating populations or subspecies of red deer. Replacing it with histone-gene DNA would not be expected to improve differentiation.

**C — False.** The smallest bootstrap value shown anywhere on the tree is 50, so the branch marked 50 is the most weakly supported displayed branch. Sample 163 occurs in a different, lower subtree and is not a descendant of that branch. It therefore is not part of the tree's least-supported clade.

**D — False.** A bootstrap value of 50 says only that the corresponding branch, and hence its clade, appeared in 50 of the 100 resampled analyses. In the remaining 50 analyses the taxa may have been arranged among several different topologies. The value gives their combined frequency; it neither specifies the alternatives nor implies that exactly one alternative topology exists.

## Task 3 — Cell-cycle control of DNA double-strand-break repair by BRCA1 and 53BP1

**Official answer: A — True; B — True; C — False; D — True.**

### Reasoning

**A — True.** Figure 2 shows BRCA1 abundance changing as the cells progress through the cell cycle: it rises as the population moves from G1 through S toward G2/M and later falls as the population becomes G1-dominated again. This matters for pathway choice because BRCA1 is required to activate homologous recombination (HR) and inhibits 53BP1, which otherwise promotes non-homologous end joining (NHEJ). The cell-cycle-dependent change in BRCA1 abundance therefore shifts the balance between HR and NHEJ, so repair-pathway choice correlates with cell-cycle status.

**B — True.** Without BRCA1, HR cannot be activated, irrespective of whether 53BP1 is present. BRCA1-deficient cells must therefore depend more heavily on the remaining NHEJ route to repair DSBs and survive. Their 53BP1 is still present, stays at a relatively constant level through the cell cycle, and is no longer inhibited by BRCA1, allowing it to promote NHEJ.

**C — False.** Figure 2 shows the opposite overall relationship: BRCA1 reaches its highest abundance around the G2/M-rich part of the cycle and is lower during the G1 periods. Because BRCA1 is required for HR, this indicates less HR activity in G1 than in G2, not more. This also accords with HR being favored after DNA replication, when a sister chromatid is available as a homologous repair template.

**D — True.** Blocking the G1-to-S transition keeps cells in G1. In 53BP1-deficient cells, NHEJ is impaired, so survival depends on BRCA1-mediated HR; however, BRCA1 is scarce in G1, and a replicated sister chromatid is not yet available, making HR inefficient. BRCA1-deficient cells cannot use HR, but their stable, uninhibited 53BP1 can still support NHEJ during G1. A G1 arrest therefore removes effective DSB-repair capacity more severely from 53BP1-deficient cells than from BRCA1-deficient cells.

## Task 4 — Calcium-channel control of mitochondrial calcium in pancreatic beta cells

**Official answer: A — True; B — False; C — False; D — False.**

### Reasoning

**A — True.** Raising the extracellular K⁺ concentration from 5 mM to 30 mM reduces the K⁺ concentration gradient across the beta-cell membrane. Less K⁺ leaves the cell, so the membrane potential becomes less negative: the cell depolarizes. The mitochondrial Ca²⁺ transients that begin when the 30 mM K⁺ treatment is applied are consistent with depolarization initiating the calcium response.

**B — False.** The experiment activates the response by depolarizing the cells with high K⁺, not by adding a ligand for Channel A. Blocking Channel A strongly reduces the K⁺-induced mitochondrial Ca²⁺ peak, which is consistent with Channel A being a voltage-gated calcium channel that opens upon depolarization. The data therefore do not support the claim that it is ligand-gated.

**C — False.** High glucose is expected to raise calcium in a pancreatic beta cell. Glucose metabolism increases the ATP-to-ADP ratio, causing ATP-sensitive K⁺ channels to close and the plasma membrane to depolarize. Depolarization opens voltage-gated Channel A, Ca²⁺ enters the cytosol, and this influx can promote further release from the ER and transfer of Ca²⁺ into mitochondria. Thus replacing the high-K⁺ stimulus with high glucose should still produce an increase in mitochondrial Ca²⁺ rather than no increase.

**D — False.** Removing extracellular Ca²⁺ is not the same experimental manipulation as selectively blocking Channel A while leaving 2 mM Ca²⁺ in the medium. In Ca²⁺-free medium, extracellular Ca²⁺ is unavailable to every possible influx route, and the altered influx also changes activation and refilling of intracellular calcium stores. By contrast, inhibitor Y targets only Channel A, while ER release and any other calcium routes can still contribute to the mitochondrial signal. Because mitochondrial Ca²⁺ integrates these several sources, the control trace in 0 mM extracellular Ca²⁺ cannot be expected to reproduce the inhibitor-Y trace.

## Task 5 — Ketogenic-diet seizure protection, GABA production, and catecholamines

**Official answer: A — True; B — False; C — True; D — False.**

### Reasoning

**A — True.** Ketolysis ultimately supplies acetyl-CoA, which combines with oxaloacetate to make citrate in reaction 2. Increased use of oxaloacetate in this reaction lowers its availability. The coupled transamination shown in Figure 1 can be written as

$$
\text{aspartate}+\alpha\text{-ketoglutarate}
\rightleftharpoons
\text{oxaloacetate}+\text{glutamate}.
$$

Removing oxaloacetate by citrate formation pulls this equilibrium to the right. A ketogenic diet therefore favors conversion of alpha-ketoglutarate into glutamate; that glutamate can subsequently be converted to the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA in reaction 3.

**B — False.** A lower $K_m$ generally means that the reaction-2 enzyme has greater effective affinity for its substrate and, at a substrate concentration below saturation with unchanged $V_{\max}$, a higher rate. Faster reaction 2 would consume more oxaloacetate, drive the transamination above toward glutamate, and make more substrate available for GABA production. Because GABA reduces neuronal excitability, the intended prediction is greater resistance to fluorothyl-induced seizures and hence a **longer**, not shorter, latency to the first seizure in ketogenic-diet $Dbh^{+/-}$ mice. The proposed decrease in latency therefore has the direction reversed.

**C — True.** L-DOPA is converted into dopamine, so it can replenish dopamine when endogenous dopamine production is inadequate, as in Parkinson's disease. In animals with functional DBH, dopamine can also be converted into noradrenaline and then adrenaline. Figure 2 shows that the ketogenic diet significantly lengthens seizure latency in $Dbh^{+/-}$ mice, which retain this downstream pathway, but not in $Dbh^{-/-}$ mice, which cannot make noradrenaline or adrenaline from dopamine. The intended inference is therefore that L-DOPA can supply precursor for a catecholamine-dependent anti-seizure effect as well as supply dopamine for Parkinson's treatment. This is the official basis for marking C true, although the experiment did not administer L-DOPA itself and thus does not, by itself, establish L-DOPA as a clinical anticonvulsant.

**D — False.** Removing DBH eliminates noradrenaline and also prevents production of adrenaline, because adrenaline lies downstream of noradrenaline. The loss of the ketogenic diet's significant effect in $Dbh^{-/-}$ mice therefore cannot identify which of these two catecholamines is required. Moreover, the experiment measures seizure latency, not GABA concentration or a direct interaction between noradrenaline and GABA signaling. It supports a dependence of ketogenic-diet protection on the DBH-dependent catecholamine branch, but it is not conclusive evidence that noradrenaline specifically enhances GABA's anti-seizure action.

## Task 6 — ProQC toehold-switch control of full-length mRNA translation

**Official answer: A — True; B — True; C — True; D — True.**

### Reasoning

**A — True.** When the toehold-switch sequence is unbound, it folds into a hairpin that hides the ribosome-binding site (RBS), so a ribosome cannot efficiently initiate translation of the gene of interest (GOI). A complete ProQC transcript also contains the reverse-complementary cis-trigger at its $3'$ end. Binding of that cis-trigger to the switch replaces the inhibitory switch hairpin with a switch–trigger duplex, exposes the RBS, and turns translation on. Because a prematurely terminated transcript lacks the downstream cis-trigger, its RBS normally remains hidden. This is the intended meaning of “only” in A: translation from the standard cassette is conditional on formation of the switch–trigger duplex.

**B — True.** Ordinary expression from incomplete mRNAs can consume amino acids, energy, and ribosome time while producing shortened, usually nonfunctional proteins. A transcript that has lost its stop codon can also leave a ribosome stalled at its end and make ribosome recycling more difficult. ProQC suppresses initiation on such truncated transcripts because they lack the $3'$ cis-trigger needed to expose the $5'$ RBS. Full-length transcripts contain both the trigger and the stop codon, so they can be translated to completion and release the ribosome normally. ProQC therefore directs a larger share of the cell's expression resources toward complete, functional product and reduces waste.

**C — True (official answer, with a qualification).** The intended comparison is with prokaryotic cells, where transcription and translation occur in the same compartment and can be coupled. A ribosome can therefore encounter an RNA while it is still being made, making a device that withholds the RBS until the $3'$ end has been transcribed especially useful. In eukaryotes, transcription and pre-mRNA processing occur in the nucleus, whereas translation occurs in the cytoplasm. The $5'$ cap, splicing, $3'$ cleavage and polyadenylation, nuclear retention, and export surveillance generally keep unfinished pre-mRNAs away from cytoplasmic ribosomes, so the exam treats ProQC as unnecessary there. The absolute wording is biologically stronger than the evidence warrants: eukaryotic quality control is not perfect, and aberrant or damaged cytoplasmic mRNAs can still be translated. Thus the official **True** is best understood as the simplified, intended contrast between coupled prokaryotic expression and compartmentalized eukaryotic expression, not as a claim that ProQC could never benefit a eukaryotic synthetic system.

**D — True.** Opening the switch depends on complementary base pairing, not on the trigger being covalently attached to the same RNA molecule. A separately transcribed trigger RNA with the appropriate complementary sequence can bind the toehold switch in trans, disrupt the RBS-sequestering hairpin, and expose the RBS for translation initiation. This also clarifies the scope of A: a trans-trigger can physically substitute for the built-in cis-trigger, so A's word “only” should not be read as excluding every separately supplied complementary trigger.

## Task 7 — DNA fiber assays: replication-fork progression, termination, and origin firing

**Official answer: A — True; B — True; C — False; D — False.**

### Reasoning

**A — True.** CldU was supplied first and appears red, whereas IdU was supplied second and appears blue. A fork that remains active through both pulses therefore leaves an older red tract followed, in its direction of travel, by a newer blue tract. Panel A has this sequence at only one end of the red tract, so it can be produced by one progressing fork. Panel B instead has a central red tract with blue extensions at both ends. This is the expected pattern when an origin launches two forks in opposite directions: both forks synthesize red-labeled DNA during the first pulse and then extend outward with blue-labeled DNA during the second pulse. (The official explanation calls IdU green, but the question and Figure 1 identify it as blue; this color-name mismatch does not affect the interpretation.)

**B — True.** In panel C, the first-pulse red tracts lie outside the second-pulse blue tracts. Thus a fork from the left moved rightward from red into blue, while a fork from the right moved leftward from red into blue. Their later blue tracts meet in the middle, the characteristic pattern of two converging forks completing replication of the intervening region. Panel E contains a red tract but no subsequent blue extension, so synthesis on that tract ceased before or at the change to the IdU pulse; the official key counts this as a termination pattern. There is, however, a genuine ambiguity in the official marking: a red-only tract can also result from a stalled or collapsed fork, as the official explanation itself acknowledges. Panel E therefore does not uniquely *demonstrate* normal replication termination. The answer here retains the required official **True**, but the word “demonstrate” is stronger than the evidence supports.

**C — False.** CldU is a thymidine analogue available to every DNA polymerase during the first pulse; it does not selectively label only one mode of strand synthesis. At each replication fork, one daughter strand is synthesized continuously as the leading strand and the other discontinuously as the lagging strand, and both incorporate CldU. Because panel B represents two forks moving away from an origin, first-pulse labeling occurs in both leading- and lagging-strand products, not exclusively in one or the other.

**D — False.** Panel F has the sequence blue–red–blue–red–blue along one uninterrupted DNA molecule. Each red first-pulse region with later blue extension on both sides is an origin-firing signature, so the panel records two origins on the same molecule. Under the standard chromosome-replication model used here, a bacterial chromosome initiates from one origin, whereas a eukaryotic chromosome uses many origins. Panel F is therefore inconsistent with the proposed bacterial origin of the DNA and supports the official False classification.

## Task 8 — Inferring nuclease polarity and DNA-polymerase activity from denaturing PAGE

**Official answer: A — False; B — True; C — True; D — False.**

### Reasoning

**A — False.** Reaction 1 establishes the positions of the intact labeled strands: the red-labeled lower strand is 100 nucleotides long, whereas the blue-labeled upper strand is 50 nucleotides long. In reaction 2, enzyme A converts both into shorter, discrete products of approximately 27 and 22 nucleotides. This pattern is consistent with endonucleolytic cleavage at defined internal sites. However, a blunt cut would cleave the paired strands at the same position, so the two labeled fragments, measured from the same end of the duplex, would have equal lengths and would appear as one purple band. Instead, their lengths differ by about five nucleotides, demonstrating staggered cuts. On the labeled left-hand product, the lower strand projects beyond the upper strand and its projecting terminus is a 5′ end, so A produces a 5′ overhang rather than a blunt end. The statement as a whole is therefore false even though A is an endonuclease.

**B — True.** In reaction 3, the red-labeled 100-nucleotide strand remains intact, while the blue-labeled strand changes from a sharp 50-nucleotide band into a smear of shorter products. A smear is expected from progressive removal of nucleotides by an exonuclease. The blue fluorophore blocks the upper strand's 5′ terminus, whereas its unlabeled 3′ terminus remains available for degradation. The shortening of that strand must therefore proceed from 3′ toward 5′. The red label similarly makes the lower strand's 3′ end chemically inert, explaining why that strand is not shortened.

**C — True.** In reaction 4, C acts after A has made the staggered cut. The shorter blue-labeled product has a free 3′ end paired with the longer red-labeled fragment, so C can extend the blue strand by about five nucleotides. Both products then have the same approximately 27-nucleotide length and appear as a purple band. In reaction 5, C similarly extends the blue strands left by exonuclease B along the intact 100-nucleotide red strand, producing coincident 100-nucleotide products. Template-directed addition to a primer's 3′ end is DNA-polymerase activity, so enzyme C is a DNA polymerase.

**D — False.** The original substrate already has an extendable 3′ end on the 50-nucleotide upper strand, while the lower strand continues for another 50 nucleotides and can serve as its template. Enzyme C alone would therefore extend the upper strand to 100 nucleotides, placing the blue and red signals together at 100 nucleotides, as in reaction 5. It would not reproduce reaction 4's approximately 27-nucleotide purple product, which depends on enzyme A first creating the staggered cleavage products.

*Figure-color note: the embedded official explanation calls the upper labeled strand green, but the question text and rendered figure identify it as blue. This naming inconsistency does not affect the official truth pattern or the interpretation of the gel.*

## Task 9 — Checkpoint-kinase-dependent changes in DNA content and polyploidy

**Official answer: A — False; B — True; C — False; D — False.**

### Reasoning

**A — False.** In the wild-type control histogram, the largest peak is at 2n DNA content. Because the figure defines 2n as the DNA content of a non-dividing diploid cell, this peak represents cells in G1, before DNA replication. Cells passing through S phase have intermediate DNA contents between 2n and 4n, while cells in G2 or M have already replicated their DNA and therefore lie at 4n. Thus the control population is mainly in G1, not G2.

**B — True.** Compound X shifts the wild-type distribution toward larger genome contents: the major peak moves from 2n to 4n, and a distinct population appears at about 8n. An 8n cell contains more complete genome equivalents than an ordinary diploid cell-cycle population, whose DNA content ranges from 2n in G1 to 4n after S phase. The appearance of this higher integer-multiple peak therefore indicates an additional round of genome replication without the normal intervening reduction of DNA content by cell division, which is the evidence for compound-X-induced polyploidy.

**C — False.** The response to compound X differs sharply according to kinase status. Wild-type cells acquire the large 4n peak and an appreciable 8n population after treatment, whereas cells deficient in both kinases retain a distribution broadly similar to their untreated control and do not show the corresponding accumulation at high DNA content. The compound's effect therefore requires, or is at least mediated by, checkpoint kinases A and B; it is not kinase-independent.

**D — False.** The comparison points in the opposite direction. Following compound X treatment, cells with intact kinases A and B reach 8n DNA content, consistent with another replication cycle occurring without a normal intervening division. Kinase-deficient cells fail to make this treatment-induced shift. Thus, under the conditions tested, the checkpoint kinases promote or permit the extra genome replication associated with the response rather than preventing it.

The official explanation calls the 8n population “tetraploid.” This uses the figure's flow-cytometric DNA-content labels: a tetraploid cell that has replicated its DNA can contain 8n-equivalent DNA. In strict cytogenetic notation, ploidy (chromosome-set number) and DNA content after replication are distinct, so an 8n DNA-content label should not by itself be read as eight chromosome sets.

## Task 10 — Transcriptional and phosphorylation control of stem-cell differentiation

**Official answer: A — True; B — False; C — True; D — True.**

### Reasoning

**A — True.** Protein A is controlled through its expression. Changing its activity therefore requires transcription of RNA and translation of protein, which takes time and consumes many nucleotide triphosphates and other cellular resources. Protein B is already present and can instead be switched between phosphorylation states: a kinase can add a phosphate using ATP, and a phosphatase can remove it. Reversibly modifying an existing protein is consequently both faster and less costly than making a new RNA and protein, so it is the more energy-efficient mechanism for a rapid response.

**B — False.** The controls identify fraction I as the unbound-protein fraction because it contains actin but not histone H3, whereas fraction II contains histone H3 but not actin and is therefore the DNA-associated fraction. After ligand L is added, the transcription-factor-X band becomes weaker in fraction I and stronger in fraction II. Thus ligand L shifts transcription factor X toward the DNA-bound pool, indicating increased rather than reduced DNA binding affinity.

**C — True.** Actin is similar across the three lanes, so the differentiation-marker differences are not explained by unequal protein loading. Kinase-deficient cells have more differentiation marker than control cells, while phosphatase-deficient cells have less. Removing the kinase reduces phosphorylation of protein B and favors differentiation; removing the phosphatase keeps more B phosphorylated and disfavors differentiation. Because the problem states that differentiation requires the A–B heterodimer, these results support the conclusion that phosphorylation of B inhibits A–B association.

**D — True.** Phosphorylation places a negatively charged phosphate group on serine. Aspartate also has a negatively charged side chain, unlike the nonpolar side chain of leucine, so replacing serine-121 with aspartate is a plausible phosphomimetic substitution. The mutant should therefore resemble persistently phosphorylated protein B and should mimic the kinase's inhibitory effect on heterodimer formation. Aspartate does not reproduce every structural feature of phosphoserine, but the statement only claims that the same effect is likely.

## Task 11 — Cycloheximide-chase analysis of proteasomal and lysosomal protein degradation

**Official answer: A — False; B — True; C — True; D — False.**

### Reasoning

**A — False.** Cycloheximide blocks translation elongation on eukaryotic cytosolic 80S ribosomes. Once it is added, the experiment follows the disappearance of protein molecules that were already present while little or no replacement protein is made by those ribosomes. A decrease in abundance during this chase therefore directly reports protein turnover or degradation, not the rate at which the protein had been synthesized. Measuring synthesis would require an additional readout of newly made protein.

**B — True.** In Figure 1-a, protein B begins at an abundance of about 75 when cycloheximide is added. It falls below half of that value between 4 and 6 hours: at 6 hours its abundance is only about 34. Protein C begins near 70 but is still around 43 at 8 hours, above its half-abundance value of 35. Thus B reaches half of its initial abundance sooner than C and has the shorter half-life.

**C — True.** The prompt states that A has a shorter half-life than B, so if A depended on the same cycloheximide-sensitive 80S translation as B, its abundance should fall rapidly after translation is blocked. Instead, A remains nearly constant throughout period II. This suggests that its ongoing production uses a cycloheximide-insensitive translation system. In a human cell, mitochondrial ribosomes provide the relevant alternative, making A more likely to be a mitochondrial, and especially a mitochondrially encoded and translated, protein than B or C. The graph supports this comparative likelihood rather than proving localization: many mitochondrial proteins are nuclear encoded and translated on cytosolic ribosomes.

**D — False.** For protein B in Figure 1-b, the proteasome inhibitor MG132 largely prevents the loss of protein, whereas chloroquine gives almost the same decline as the untreated control. B is therefore degraded mainly by proteasomes. For protein C, chloroquine prevents much of the decline, while MG132 has little effect, indicating mainly lysosomal degradation. Proteasomes chiefly destroy intracellular cytosolic or nuclear substrates, whereas extracellular proteins taken up by endocytosis can be delivered to lysosomes. Consequently, C—not B—is the more plausible extracellular protein.

## Task 12 — Förster resonance energy transfer in protease and inhibitor assays

**Official answer: A — False; B — False; C — True; D — True.**

### Reasoning

**A — False.** FRET requires an excited donor to come within roughly 10 nm of a spectrally compatible acceptor. Increasing the concentrations of both freely dispersed chromophores raises their number densities and reduces the typical distance from a donor to a nearby acceptor. ## Task 15 — Lotka–Volterra competition isoclines and population outcomes

**Official answer: A — True; B — True; C — True; D — False.**

### Reasoning

**A — True.** For a nonzero population of species 1, its zero-growth condition is

\[
K_1-N_1-\alpha N_2=0,
\]

so its isocline can be written as \(N_1=K_1-\alpha N_2\), or \(N_2=(K_1-N_1)/\alpha\). At the \(N_2\)-axis, \(N_1=0\); hence the intercept is \(N_2=K_1/\alpha\), exactly as stated.

**B — True.** Each species' own abundance appears as a negative term in its per-capita growth factor: \(-N_1\) in the equation for species 1 and \(-N_2\) in that for species 2. Thus, even in the absence of the other species, growth slows as a population approaches its own carrying capacity. Those terms represent intraspecific competition, whereas \(-\alpha N_2\) and \(-\beta N_1\) represent interspecific competition.

**C — True.** Let elks be species 1 and deer be species 2. Their nontrivial zero-growth lines are

\[
N_2=200-N_1 \quad\text{(elks)},
\qquad
N_2=100-2N_1 \quad\text{(deer)}.
\]

The elk line has intercepts \((N_1,N_2)=(200,0)\) and \((0,200)\), while the deer line has intercepts \((50,0)\) and \((0,100)\). The elk isocline therefore lies outside the deer isocline throughout the biologically relevant first quadrant, the competitive-exclusion arrangement in which species 1 wins. The signs at the stated initial point \((120,60)\) confirm the direction immediately:

\[
K_1-N_1-\alpha N_2=200-120-60=20>0,
\]

so elks increase, whereas

\[
K_2-N_2-\beta N_1=100-60-2(120)=-200<0,
\]

so deer decrease. The trajectory consequently approaches the elk-only equilibrium \((N_1,N_2)=(200,0)\): elks outcompete deer.

**D — False.** Two of the four isocline arrangements contain an intersection in the positive quadrant, but an intersection is not necessarily a stable coexistence point. In one crossing arrangement, each species limits its own growth more strongly than it limits the other species, and trajectories converge to the intersection; this is stable coexistence. In the other, the intersection is unstable: which species survives depends on the initial populations, and trajectories move away from the intersection toward exclusion of one species. The two remaining, noncrossing arrangements also lead to competitive exclusion. Therefore only **one**, not two, of the four scenarios gives stable coexistence of both species.

\qquad

## Task 40 — Pharmacokinetics: first-pass activation, distribution, protein binding, and enterohepatic cycling

**Official answer: A — True; B — True; C — True; D — True.**

### Reasoning

**A — True.** After absorption from the intestine, an orally administered compound enters the hepatic portal circulation and passes through the liver before reaching the general circulation. For a prodrug whose liver metabolism produces the active molecule, extensive first-pass conversion can therefore be useful: a large fraction is activated during this first transit. Under the question's intended comparison of otherwise similar prodrugs, oral delivery is consequently more suitable for one that is efficiently activated by first-pass metabolism than for one that undergoes little such activation.

**B — True.** Only the unbound fraction of a drug can readily leave the circulation and enter most tissues. Strong binding to albumin or another plasma protein retains much of the drug in the vascular compartment. The apparent volume of distribution is

$$
V_d=\frac{\text{amount of drug in the body}}{\text{plasma drug concentration}}.
$$

Retention in plasma makes the measured plasma concentration relatively high for a given total amount of drug, so $V_d$ is small—potentially approaching the roughly 3 L plasma volume in the simplified model used by the official explanation. A drug that enters interstitial fluid and cells extensively instead has a larger apparent $V_d$.

**C — True.** Plasma-protein binding creates a reversible reservoir. While drug molecules are bound, they are generally unavailable for diffusion into hepatocytes, metabolism by liver enzymes, and glomerular filtration; as free drug is removed, some bound drug dissociates to replace it. An unbound lipophilic drug can cross membranes readily and be delivered to well-perfused organs such as the liver, where it can be metabolized and then eliminated. With the other pharmacokinetic factors treated as equal, the unbound drug is therefore cleared more rapidly and is expected to have the shorter half-life. This is consistent with the relation

$$
t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln 2\,V_d}{CL},
$$

because reduced clearance $CL$ tends to prolong half-life.

**D — True.** Oestrogens can be conjugated in the liver and secreted into bile. In the intestine, microbial enzymes can hydrolyse some of those conjugates, releasing oestrogen that can be absorbed again and returned through the portal blood. In the simplified description in Table 1, only 5% is finally excreted on a pass, leaving 95% available for reabsorption and another cycle. Repeated liver–bile–intestine–blood cycling thus delays final loss in faeces, can produce secondary rises in plasma concentration, and prolongs both residence time and biological activity of oestrogen-containing oral contraceptives.

**Pharmacokinetic qualifications.** The official verdicts follow the simplified tendencies stated in the task, but A–C are not unconditional rules. Extensive first-pass metabolism benefits oral administration only when it predictably forms the desired active metabolite rather than destroying the drug or producing harmful metabolites. Strong plasma-protein binding tends to lower $V_d$, but extensive tissue binding can counteract that tendency. For C, binding can change both clearance and $V_d$, while an unbound lipophilic drug may be stored in adipose tissue; its actual half-life therefore cannot be inferred from protein binding alone without information about tissue partitioning, hepatic extraction, and renal handling. Finally, enterohepatic recycling of oestrogen depends on biliary secretion of metabolites and their intestinal deconjugation and reabsorption; the official explanation's appeal to a structural resemblance between oestrogen, cholesterol, and bile salts is not by itself the mechanism.

## Task 42 — Starling forces, capillary filtration, and liver sinusoids

**Official answer: A — True; B — True; C — True; D — True.**

### Reasoning

**A — True.** Prolonged severe protein deficiency can reduce the concentration of circulating proteins, especially albumin, and therefore lower the capillary oncotic pressure, $\pi_c$. In the Starling equation,

$$
J_v=K_f\left[(P_c-P_i)-\sigma(\pi_c-\pi_i)\right],
$$

the oncotic term normally opposes outward filtration. If $\pi_c$ falls while the other quantities remain fixed, $(\pi_c-\pi_i)$ becomes smaller, so a smaller inward oncotic force is subtracted from the outward hydrodynamic force. Equivalently, $\Delta J_v=-K_f\sigma\Delta\pi_c>0$ when $\Delta\pi_c<0$. Net filtration therefore tends to increase, which can contribute to oedema if lymphatic return cannot remove the extra interstitial fluid.

**B — True.** The filtration coefficient $K_f$ reflects both the hydraulic permeability of the capillary wall and the surface area available for exchange. Capillary-wall damage, widened intercellular gaps, or other loss of integrity can directly alter how readily water crosses the wall and thus alter $K_f$; damage commonly increases permeability and $K_f$. The statement does not require the change to have one particular direction.

**C — True.** For changes in the pressures in the model given,

$$
\Delta P_c=\frac{0.2\Delta P_a+\Delta P_v}{1.2}.
$$

Thus a $1$-unit rise in arterial pressure contributes only $0.2/1.2=1/6$ unit to $P_c$, whereas a $1$-unit rise in venous pressure contributes $1/1.2=5/6$ unit. The latter effect is five times as large. Venous constriction tends to raise pressure upstream on the capillary side, so the intended simple-model conclusion is that it has the more pronounced effect on capillary pressure.

**D — True.** Liver capillaries are highly permeable sinusoids. Their discontinuous, fenestrated endothelium permits large solutes, including proteins secreted by hepatocytes, to pass between sinusoidal blood and the space around the liver cells. Consequently, proteins are poorly reflected by this wall: $\sigma$ is low, and protein movement also makes the difference $(\pi_c-\pi_i)$ relatively small. The oncotic contribution $\sigma(\pi_c-\pi_i)$ is therefore weak, leaving the hydrodynamic pressure difference $(P_c-P_i)$ as the main driver of fluid exchange.

**Qualifications.** The embedded official explanation for A has a sign error in its wording: lowering $\pi_c$ makes the negative oncotic contribution less negative and the total Starling driving pressure more positive, not more negative. Its conclusion and official truth value are nevertheless correct. A low-protein diet increases filtration only insofar as it causes hypoproteinaemia; the question's long duration and the word “tends” supply that intended assumption. For C, the displayed equation establishes a fivefold difference for equal changes in $P_a$ and $P_v$, but it does not by itself show that equal degrees of arterial and venous constriction produce equal-sized pressure changes. Predicting that would require vascular resistances and boundary pressures, so the official verdict relies on the question's simplified pressure-based interpretation.

In E, however, DNP is present during the same hypoosmotic exposure, and activity remains only about $20\%$ even 48 hours after the animals have been returned to normal seawater; its 72-hour value is significantly below that of B. DNP alone in D also permits near-complete recovery. The persistent effect specifically seen in the combined treatment therefore supports the conclusion that ATP-dependent processes—such as restoration of ion and cell-volume balance—are crucial for tolerating and recovering from hypoosmotic stress.

**Data-interpretation qualification.** DNP is an experimental proxy for impaired mitochondrial ATP production, not a selective removal of ATP, and as a protonophore it can have additional physiological effects. Moreover, the *shi-ts* mutation, which disrupts vesicle endocytosis and exocytosis in the DA neuron, raises arousal from about 27% to about 70% (Figure 1D). Neurotransmitter secretion requires presynaptic excitation: depolarization and an action potential open voltage-gated calcium channels, and calcium entry triggers synaptic-vesicle fusion. The results consequently support the intended model in which CCHa1 excites the DA neuron and promotes dopamine release, thereby preventing arousal.

## Task 50 — Maternal-effect patterning of the Drosophila body axes

**Official answer: A — False; B — False; C — True; D — True.**

### Reasoning

**A — False.** Bicoid is a maternal-effect determinant: during oogenesis, the mother loads bicoid mRNA into the oocyte and localizes it at the anterior pole. After fertilization, translation of that maternal mRNA produces the anterior-to-posterior Bicoid protein gradient that specifies anterior structures, with high Bicoid concentrations required for head and acron fates and lower concentrations contributing to more posterior fates such as thorax. Depleting bicoid in the father's testes does not remove the bicoid mRNA already supplied by the mother to the egg. A paternal depletion therefore would not produce the stated headless, thorax-deficient phenotype; bicoid would have to be depleted from the female germ line or oocyte to disrupt this early axis.

**B — False.** Exuperantia normally holds bicoid mRNA at the anterior of the oocyte. In a loss-of-function mutant, the mRNA can spread instead of remaining concentrated at that pole, so the embryo cannot form the normal steep Bicoid protein gradient. Figure 1B indicates that the acron occupies the extreme, highest-Bicoid end of the concentration range. Dispersing the maternal mRNA lowers that anterior peak below the threshold needed for acron and head specification; it does not extend the acron. The expected change is loss of the acron and head, with expansion of lower-threshold, more posterior identities such as thorax.

**C — True.** The reciprocal tissue combinations distinguish where Torpedo is needed. A torpedo-deficient oocyte surrounded by wild-type follicle cells develops a normal body plan, whereas a wild-type oocyte surrounded by torpedo-deficient follicle cells produces an entirely ventralized embryo. Thus the decisive Torpedo activity is in the somatic follicle cells that receive the oocyte's Gurken signal, not in the oocyte itself. Torpedo expression in the oocyte is therefore unnecessary for establishment of the normal dorsal–ventral axis under the conditions described.

**D — True.** Loss of Torpedo from all follicle cells eliminates Gurken–Torpedo signaling and gives an entirely ventral embryo. The side with strong Gurken signaling must therefore be the side that acquires or induces dorsal identity, while the weak-signal side corresponds to ventral identity. In Figure 1C, Gurken concentration is highest in the dark region at the bottom and decreases toward the light region at the top; the arrow points upward from high to low concentration. It consequently points from the future dorsal side toward the future ventral side.
