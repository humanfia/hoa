# Part B — Task 11

*Cycloheximide-chase analysis of proteasomal and lysosomal protein degradation*

**Official answer: A — False; B — True; C — True; D — False.**

## Reasoning

**A — False.** Cycloheximide blocks translation elongation on eukaryotic cytosolic 80S ribosomes. Once it is added, the experiment follows the disappearance of protein molecules that were already present while little or no replacement protein is made by those ribosomes. A decrease in abundance during this chase therefore directly reports protein turnover or degradation, not the rate at which the protein had been synthesized. Measuring synthesis would require an additional readout of newly made protein.

**B — True.** In Figure 1-a, protein B begins at an abundance of about 75 when cycloheximide is added. It falls below half of that value between 4 and 6 hours: at 6 hours its abundance is only about 34. Protein C begins near 70 but is still around 43 at 8 hours, above its half-abundance value of 35. Thus B reaches half of its initial abundance sooner than C and has the shorter half-life.

**C — True.** The prompt states that A has a shorter half-life than B, so if A depended on the same cycloheximide-sensitive 80S translation as B, its abundance should fall rapidly after translation is blocked. Instead, A remains nearly constant throughout period II. This suggests that its ongoing production uses a cycloheximide-insensitive translation system. In a human cell, mitochondrial ribosomes provide the relevant alternative, making A more likely to be a mitochondrial, and especially a mitochondrially encoded and translated, protein than B or C. The graph supports this comparative likelihood rather than proving localization: many mitochondrial proteins are nuclear encoded and translated on cytosolic ribosomes.

**D — False.** For protein B in Figure 1-b, the proteasome inhibitor MG132 largely prevents the loss of protein, whereas chloroquine gives almost the same decline as the untreated control. B is therefore degraded mainly by proteasomes. For protein C, chloroquine prevents much of the decline, while MG132 has little effect, indicating mainly lysosomal degradation. Proteasomes chiefly destroy intracellular cytosolic or nuclear substrates, whereas extracellular proteins taken up by endocytosis can be delivered to lysosomes. Consequently, C—not B—is the more plausible extracellular protein.
