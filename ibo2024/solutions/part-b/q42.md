# Part B — Task 42

*Starling forces, capillary filtration, and liver sinusoids*

**Official answer: A — True; B — True; C — True; D — True.**

## Reasoning

**A — True.** Prolonged severe protein deficiency can reduce the concentration of circulating proteins, especially albumin, and therefore lower the capillary oncotic pressure, $\pi_c$. In the Starling equation,

$$
J_v=K_f\left[(P_c-P_i)-\sigma(\pi_c-\pi_i)\right],
$$

the oncotic term normally opposes outward filtration. If $\pi_c$ falls while the other quantities remain fixed, $(\pi_c-\pi_i)$ becomes smaller, so a smaller inward oncotic force is subtracted from the outward hydrodynamic force. Equivalently, $\Delta J_v=-K_f\sigma\Delta\pi_c>0$ when $\Delta\pi_c<0$. Net filtration therefore tends to increase, which can contribute to oedema if lymphatic return cannot remove the extra interstitial fluid.

**B — True.** The filtration coefficient $K_f$ reflects both the hydraulic permeability of the capillary wall and the surface area available for exchange. Capillary-wall damage, widened intercellular gaps, or other loss of integrity can directly alter how readily water crosses the wall and thus alter $K_f$; damage commonly increases permeability and $K_f$. The statement does not require the change to have one particular direction.

**C — True.** For changes in the pressures in the model given,

$$
\Delta P_c=\frac{0.2\Delta P_a+\Delta P_v}{1.2}.
$$

Thus a $1$-unit rise in arterial pressure contributes only $0.2/1.2=1/6$ unit to $P_c$, whereas a $1$-unit rise in venous pressure contributes $1/1.2=5/6$ unit. The latter effect is five times as large. Venous constriction tends to raise pressure upstream on the capillary side, so the intended simple-model conclusion is that it has the more pronounced effect on capillary pressure.

**D — True.** Liver capillaries are highly permeable sinusoids. Their discontinuous, fenestrated endothelium permits large solutes, including proteins secreted by hepatocytes, to pass between sinusoidal blood and the space around the liver cells. Consequently, proteins are poorly reflected by this wall: $\sigma$ is low, and protein movement also makes the difference $(\pi_c-\pi_i)$ relatively small. The oncotic contribution $\sigma(\pi_c-\pi_i)$ is therefore weak, leaving the hydrodynamic pressure difference $(P_c-P_i)$ as the main driver of fluid exchange.

**Qualifications.** The embedded official explanation for A has a sign error in its wording: lowering $\pi_c$ makes the negative oncotic contribution less negative and the total Starling driving pressure more positive, not more negative. Its conclusion and official truth value are nevertheless correct. A low-protein diet increases filtration only insofar as it causes hypoproteinaemia; the question's long duration and the word “tends” supply that intended assumption. For C, the displayed equation establishes a fivefold difference for equal changes in $P_a$ and $P_v$, but it does not by itself show that equal degrees of arterial and venous constriction produce equal-sized pressure changes. Predicting that would require vascular resistances and boundary pressures, so the official verdict relies on the question's simplified pressure-based interpretation.
