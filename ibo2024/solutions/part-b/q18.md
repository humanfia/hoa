# Part B — Task 18

*Ant–acacia mutualism, megaherbivory, and lion prey selection*

**Official answer: A — False; B — False; C — False; D — True.**

## Reasoning

**A — False.** The ants clearly benefit because the whistling-thorn trees supply them with food and shelter, but Figure 1A shows that the trees benefit as well. When megaherbivores are present, plots invaded by big-headed ants have much greater visibility than uninvaded plots. Because greater visibility means fewer whistling-thorn trees, removal of the resident acacia ants allows elephants to damage or eliminate more trees. The acacia ants therefore protect their host from herbivory. Both partners benefit, so this is mutualism rather than commensalism.

**B — False.** Big-headed ants exterminate the protective acacia ants. In the presence of megaherbivores this reduces tree cover and produces the high-visibility conditions shown for invaded plots in Figure 1A. Figure 1B shows the opposite relationship between visibility and zebra kills: the probability that lions kill a zebra is high at low visibility and falls steeply as visibility increases. Thus invasion makes zebras less, not more, vulnerable to lion predation through its effect on vegetation cover.

**C — False.** The rise of big-headed ants from 2000 to 2020 would remove more acacia ants, weaken tree protection from elephants, and reduce the area covered by whistling-thorn trees. In Figure 1C, greater tree coverage is associated with a larger annual proportion of zebra kills and a smaller proportion of buffalo kills. Decreasing coverage therefore predicts the reverse change: fewer zebra kills and more buffalo kills. Lions should shift from zebras toward buffalos, not from buffalos toward zebras.

**D — True.** Figure 1B shows that zebra-kill probability is greatest where visibility is low. Dense vegetation lets lions approach zebras while concealed and attack from short range, whereas open, high-visibility habitat gives zebras a better chance to detect lions and would require a longer pursuit. The data therefore support ambush over long-distance chasing as the more effective and preferred hunting mode for zebras.

**Discrepancy in the official explanation.** Its discussion of D says that fewer zebras are killed at lower visibility, but Figure 1B shows the reverse: kill probability decreases as visibility increases. The graph supports the official **True** key because the higher kill probability under low visibility is evidence for ambush hunting.
