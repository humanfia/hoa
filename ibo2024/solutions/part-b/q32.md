# Part B — Task 32

*Temperature, chemical inhibition, and respiratory costs of Venus flytrap closure*

**Official answer: A — True; B — False; C — False; D — True.**

## Reasoning

The official answer section presents four unlettered verdict bullets with irregular punctuation. Reading them in statement order gives **T/F/F/T**. Its final explanation also refers to Figure 1C, although statement D concerns Figure 1D; this is evidently a figure-reference error and does not change the official verdict.

**A — True.** In Figure 1A, a decrease in \(y/y_{\max}\) means that the two lobes are moving closer together. Curve 1 therefore represents the faster closure: it falls steeply to about 0.2 within roughly 0.3 s, whereas curve 2 decreases more gradually. Figure 1B shows the same experiment as closure speed and identifies the high, sharp speed peak (about \(11\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\)) as the 20°C response; the 36°C response has the smaller peak. Thus curve 1 was obtained at 20°C and curve 2 at 36°C.

**B — False.** Curve 3 in Figure 1C was recorded after 4.5 hours of treatment with substance A and shows strong inhibition of the response. Curve 4 was recorded after the treated soil had been washed repeatedly with distilled water; the prompt peak in closure speed then returned. Removal of the substance therefore restores the response, showing that its effect is reversible rather than irreversible.

**C — False.** Substance A changes both the onset and the rate of closure. In the treated trace (curve 3), the speed does not begin to rise until about 0.5 s after stimulation, while the washed trace (curve 4) rises at about 0.1 s. The treated trap also reaches a peak of only about \(1.5\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), compared with about \(10\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) after washing—roughly a six- to sevenfold difference. Substance A therefore both delays closure and lowers its speed.

**D — True.** Figure 1D concerns repeated trigger-hair stimulation of traps that are already closed, so they cannot undergo another large mechanical closure. Nevertheless, respiration rises from roughly \(0.5\) to \(3.5\ \mathrm{\mu mol\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}\) after stimulation begins, remains elevated during stimulation, and decays only gradually after stimulation ends at about 300 s. Thus the respiratory increase cannot be attributed solely to the mechanical work of closing the trap. Electrical signalling, ion pumping, and subsequent metabolic recovery or other stimulation-induced processes also consume energy.
