# Part B — Task 17

*Wolbachia-based incompatible insect technique and mosquito dispersal*

**Official answer: A — True; B — True; C — False; D — True.**

## Reasoning

**A — True.** IIT suppresses the population when a released *Wolbachia*-infected male mates with an uninfected wild female: the infected sperm and uninfected egg are cytoplasmically incompatible, so their embryos die. Because males are the active gamete dispersers in this species, a longer-lived infected male has a longer opportunity to disperse and mate with wild females. All else being equal, longer average male survival should therefore increase the number of incompatible matings and make IIT more effective. This need not be a perfectly linear relationship, but the expected direction is positive.

**B — True.** Irradiation is meant to prevent accidentally released infected females from reproducing. If the dose is too low, some of those females may remain fertile. An infected female can produce viable offspring with either an uninfected or an infected male, because the lethal combination specified in the question requires an **uninfected egg** and infected sperm. With maternal transmission of *Wolbachia*, her descendants will also tend to be infected. The infection can consequently spread through successive generations, replacing the uninfected population with females compatible with the released infected males and weakening the suppressive effect of IIT.

The wording of B is imprecise: this mechanism is population replacement, or operational resistance to the IIT program, rather than the mosquitoes evolving biological resistance to the *Wolbachia* bacterium itself. The official true designation uses “resistance” in that operational sense.

**C — False.** With random mating and an infection frequency of 0.5 in each sex, the probability of the only cytoplasmically incompatible cross is

\[
P(\text{uninfected female} \times \text{infected male})
= 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25.
\]

The other three equally frequent pairings—uninfected female with uninfected male, infected female with uninfected male, and infected female with infected male—do not undergo cytoplasmic-incompatibility-induced embryonic death. Thus 25%, not 75%, of matings fail for that reason. If an irradiated infected female were sterile, her failure to reproduce would be caused by irradiation, not by cytoplasmic incompatibility.

**D — True.** Figure 1 shows many large egg-hatch circles in the blue control site. In the adjacent red experimental site, hatch circles are most prominent near the boundary with the control and generally become smaller and sparser farther into the experimental area. This spatial gradient is consistent with fertile wild-type mosquitoes moving out of the control site. In particular, wild females already fertilized by wild males could enter the experimental site and lay viable eggs there; compatible wild male–female immigrants could do the same. Migration therefore gives the intended and most plausible explanation for the hatched eggs inside a site where the deliberately released females had been sterilized.

The map supports this interpretation but does not by itself track individual mosquitoes or exclude every alternative, such as incomplete sterilization or resident wild mosquitoes. Thus D is a well-supported inference from the spatial pattern, not a uniquely demonstrated cause.
