# Part B — Task 44

*Body-size-dependent thermoregulation and flight metabolism in bees*

**Official answer: A — True; B — True; C — False; D — False.**

## Reasoning

**A — True.** Figure 1B shows that, at the smallest body masses, the head, thorax, and abdomen of a wild-type bee are all only slightly warmer than the surrounding air. Small bees have a large surface area relative to their volume, so they exchange heat with the environment rapidly and cannot maintain a large body–air temperature difference. They consequently depend less on transferring excess heat from the thorax to warm the abdomen. Impairing that transfer in a `mut99` bee should therefore disturb whole-body heat distribution less at small body size than at large body size, for which Figure 1B shows a much larger thoracic temperature excess.

**B — True.** In Figure 1A, line W is the identity line, $T_{\mathrm{body}}=T_{\mathrm{air}}$. The wild-type abdomen follows line Y and generally remains warmer than the air because it receives heat exported from the thorax. In a large `mut99` bee, effective abdominal warming is impaired, so its abdominal temperature should move toward equilibrium with ambient air and hence toward line W. Because the mutation is described as impairing rather than completely eliminating warming, W is best understood as the expected limiting or approximate relationship, not a guarantee of exact equality at every air temperature.

**C — False.** Intensive flight produces substantial heat in the thorax. Because a `mut99` bee cannot cool the thorax effectively by exporting that heat, its thoracic temperature should rise farther than that of a normal bee. On the high-temperature side of the curve in Figure 1D—beyond the maximum near $28$–$30\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$—mass-specific metabolic rate falls as thoracic temperature rises. The intended inference is therefore that overheating limits the mutant's attainable metabolic rate rather than allowing a rate higher than that of a normally cooled bee.

**D — False.** The first premise is reasonable: for geometrically similar animals, surface area scales with length squared whereas volume, and approximately mass, scales with length cubed. A smaller bee therefore has more heat-exchanging surface per unit mass and tends to lose heat more rapidly. The proposed compensation is nevertheless contradicted by Figure 1C. The plotted log wingbeat frequency stays approximately constant across body masses, while total wing area—not wingbeat frequency—increases with mass. Thus these data do not show smaller bees compensating with a higher wingbeat frequency.

**Data-interpretation qualification.** All four graphs contain wild-type data, so A–C extrapolate from those relationships to the `mut99` phenotype rather than measuring mutant bees directly. In particular, Figure 1D is non-monotonic: metabolic rate first rises and then falls with thoracic temperature, and it does not establish the mutant's own temperature–metabolism curve. Statement C is false under the official intended assumption that intensive flight places the poorly cooled mutant on the descending, high-temperature part of the wild-type relationship; the graph alone would not prove that conclusion without the bees' actual flight temperatures and mutant metabolic measurements.
