# Part B — Task 24

*Robertsonian translocations, meiotic segregation, and human karyotypes*

**Official answer: A — False; B — True; C — True; D — True.**

## Reasoning

**A — False.** A Robertsonian translocation is a centric fusion involving two acrocentric chromosomes: their long arms join, while their very short arms are lost. Human chromosomes 1 and 2 are not acrocentric, so they cannot undergo the type of 14–21 Robertsonian fusion described here. The official explanation calls both chromosomes 1 and 2 metacentric; more precisely, chromosome 2 is commonly classified as submetacentric. This terminology issue does not alter the conclusion, because neither chromosome is acrocentric.

**B — True.** A balanced 14–21 carrier has a normal chromosome 14, a normal chromosome 21, and a fused chromosome \(\operatorname{rob}(14;21)\). These can segregate into six distinct paternal gamete types. After each fuses with a healthy maternal gamete containing one chromosome 14 and one chromosome 21, the outcomes are:

- paternal \(14+21\): normal zygote;
- paternal \(\operatorname{rob}(14;21)\): balanced carrier;
- paternal \(\operatorname{rob}(14;21)+21\): trisomy 21, producing Down syndrome;
- paternal \(\operatorname{rob}(14;21)+14\): trisomy 14, which is lethal;
- paternal \(14\) alone: monosomy 21, which is lethal;
- paternal \(21\) alone: monosomy 14, which is lethal.

Thus three of the six possible zygotic categories are lethal, so the stated fraction is \(3/6=50\%\), satisfying “not less than 50%.” This is the official solution's count of distinct possible outcomes; without information about segregation frequencies, it should not be read as a measured probability that every gamete type occurs equally often.

**C — True (official key).** Figure 1B shows a gamete with a normal chromosome 14 but no chromosome 21. Such a gamete could be produced either by chromosome-21 nondisjunction in an otherwise normal individual or by segregation in a balanced 14–21 Robertsonian-translocation carrier. Karyotyping the patient's diploid somatic cells distinguishes these immediate explanations: a fused 14–21 chromosome reveals carrier status, whereas an ordinary somatic karyotype supports a meiotic nondisjunction event. Karyotyping the parents can then show whether a detected translocation was inherited or arose de novo.

There is a qualification to the official **True** answer. The embedded explanation establishes why the patient's somatic karyotype is required, but it does not establish that the parents' karyotypes are strictly necessary to identify the immediate cause of this gamete. Parental karyotypes address the further question of the translocation's origin. Therefore the word “necessary” in the statement is broader than the official reasoning supports, although the explicit official key is retained here.

**D — True.** The five human acrocentric chromosome types are 13, 14, 15, 21, and 22. There are

\[
\binom{5}{2}=10
\]

heterologous pairings between two different chromosome types and five homologous pairings in which the two chromosome types are the same. Counting both kinds, as the official solution does, gives

\[
10+5=15
\]

possible Robertsonian-translocation types. Equivalently, this is the number of unordered pairs with repetition, \(\binom{6}{2}=15\). The official explanation's reference to “Figure 2” is an apparent numbering error; the task contains Figure 1, and Figure 1B is the relevant karyogram.
