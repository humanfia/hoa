# Part B — Task 16

*Niche dimensions, turf competition, grazing, and drought in Stipa capillata grassland*

**Official answer: A — False; B — True; C — False; D — True.**

## Reasoning

**A — False.** Rapid growth requires a rapid supply of mineral nutrients with which to build new biomass. Nutrient-poor soil therefore imposes a particularly strong limitation on fast-growing, resource-demanding plants; it does not generally allow them to overpopulate the site. Such plants are more likely to dominate nutrient-rich or newly opened sites, whereas plants adapted to low nutrient availability can retain an advantage on poor soil. In terms of the hypothesis, nutrient shortage supplies an additional limiting factor and hence a possible niche dimension, rather than automatically favouring the fastest grower.

**B — True.** In the absence of another constraint, drought-resistant *S. capillata* could become dominant. Grazing horses can remove or damage some of its above-ground biomass, reducing its height, seed production, and competitive capture of light and space. Grazing thereby adds a limiting pressure on *Stipa* and opens patches or resources for plants limited by different factors, so moderate grazing can increase the number of plant species that coexist. The statement says that grazing **can** help; severe, non-selective overgrazing need not increase diversity.

**C — False.** Dense perennial turf is the factor that prevents weak-rooted *S. capillata* from spreading vigorously: the established surface mat occupies rooting space and makes establishment difficult. Ploughing breaks up that turf and removes this competitive and physical barrier. Although ploughing can initially damage all vegetation, the resulting open soil should subsequently favour establishment and expansion of drought-resistant *Stipa*, so its biomass is not expected to decline relative to the other perennial grasses for the reason proposed.

**D — True.** Drought reduces soil-water availability. Less drought-tolerant turf plants respond by closing their stomata, which restricts carbon-dioxide entry and lowers photosynthesis; prolonged water stress therefore reduces growth and turf biomass. Meanwhile, the more drought-resistant and taller *S. capillata* can remain comparatively vigorous and shade the short plants that form the turf, further limiting their light capture and photosynthesis. Reduced photosynthetic carbon gain can thus contribute especially to loss of turf biomass during drought.
