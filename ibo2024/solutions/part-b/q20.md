# Part B — Task 20

*Female body mass, mating success, and male combat in* Gryllus bimaculatus

**Official answer: A — False; B — True; C — False; D — True.**

## Reasoning

**A — False.** In Figure 1A, females from encounters that ended in copulation (group 1) are significantly heavier than females from encounters that did not (group 2; \(p<0.05\)). The median is roughly \(1{,}000\ \mathrm{mg}\) in the copulated group but only about \(800\text{–}850\ \mathrm{mg}\) in the non-copulated group. Thus the observed association runs opposite to the statement: heavier, not lighter, females were more often in the successful-copulation group.

**B — True.** Figure 1B compares the body-mass difference from the preceding male–female encounter for males that later won (group 1) or lost (group 2) a fight. Because females are heavier on average, a smaller female–male mass difference corresponds to a relatively heavier male. Winners have a markedly smaller median difference (roughly \(150\ \mathrm{mg}\)) than losers (roughly \(300\ \mathrm{mg}\)), and the difference is significant (\(p<0.05\)). The data therefore support the conclusion that the heavier male wins more often on average.

**C — False.** Copulation time is not automatically a measure of sperm expenditure. It would be a valid proxy for depletion only after establishing that longer copulations transfer more sperm—for example, by measuring ejaculate or sperm number as a function of duration. No such relationship is provided here, and sperm transfer could occur during only part of copulation or could level off. Duration alone therefore does not quantify the sperm-related mating cost.

**D — True.** Relationship 1 in Figure 1C is monotonically increasing: additional matings raise female fitness, although the gain becomes progressively smaller. Figure 1A in turn associates larger females with successful copulation and smaller females with failure to copulate. Combining these observations, smaller females are expected to obtain fewer matings; under relationship 1, fewer matings place them lower on the fitness curve than larger females. This is consistent with sexual selection arising from male preference for larger females.
