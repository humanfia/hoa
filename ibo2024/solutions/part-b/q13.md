# Part B — Task 13

*Telomere protection, chromosome-end fusion, and chromosomal bridges*

**Official answer: A — False; B — True; C — True; D — False.**

## Reasoning

**A — False.** Figure 1B shows a telomeric loop, or t-loop: the single-stranded 3′ telomeric overhang invades the double-stranded telomeric DNA, folding the chromosome end back on itself. This hides the free DNA end from the ordinary double-strand-break repair machinery. However, the strand-invasion junction is itself a branched DNA structure resembling a recombination intermediate, so nucleases that resolve meiotic recombination intermediates can recognize and cut it. The t-loop therefore does not, by its structure alone, protect the telomere from those nucleases; it can instead provide them with a substrate.

**B — True.** In Figure 1C, chromosomes remain mostly separate when both factors are present and also when Y alone is absent. Removing X while retaining Y produces extensive end-to-end chromosome fusions, showing that X normally prevents telomeres from being treated as DNA breaks; X is therefore the more likely shelterin component. Crucially, removing Y together with X suppresses the fusion phenotype and restores mostly separate chromosomes. This means Y is required to join the exposed telomeres when X is missing, as expected for a component of a DNA-break-repair pathway such as end joining. Y is consequently more likely than X to participate in DNA-break repair.

**C — True.** A normal chromosome has one centromere, but ligation of the unprotected telomeric ends of two chromosomes creates one continuous, dicentric chromosome with a centromere from each original chromosome. If its two centromeres attach through kinetochores to opposite spindle poles, the fused chromosome is stretched into the bridge seen in Figure 1D. The bridged chromosome must therefore contain at least two centromeres.

**D — False.** Kinetochore-associated microtubules can pull the two centromeres toward opposite poles and thereby stretch the dicentric chromosome into a bridge. The time series nevertheless shows the bridge persisting after poleward chromosome movement; breakage begins only around 6 minutes, during telophase as the chromosome masses start to decondense. Because active spindle movement is no longer occurring at that stage, the observations do not support kinetochore-microtubule tension as the force that directly severs the bridge.
