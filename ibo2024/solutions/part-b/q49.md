# Part B — Task 49

*Diet-dependent gut–brain signalling and sleep arousability in fruit flies*

**Official answer: A — False; B — True; C — True; D — False.**

## Reasoning

**A — False.** In Figure 1B, regular food and food supplemented with 14 kcal of sugar both give a relative gut CCHa1 mRNA level of about 1. Adding 80 kcal of fat does not raise expression—it gives a value below 1—whereas adding only 40 kcal of protein raises expression to about 2. Thus CCHa1 expression does not follow the amount of energy added: the intermediate-calorie protein supplement has the largest effect, while the highest-calorie supplement does not. The result instead points to the nutrient's identity, particularly protein, as the relevant dietary signal.

**B — True.** Protein supplementation is the only treatment in Figure 1B that clearly increases CCHa1 expression. In Figure 1C, depleting CCHa1 raises the fraction of sleeping flies awakened by the mechanical stimulus from roughly 25% in the control to about 80%. CCHa1 therefore normally suppresses arousability. Combining the two results gives the proposed chain: more dietary protein → more gut CCHa1 → fewer flies awakened by a given vibration. In the experiment's operational sense, a protein-rich diet reduces sleep arousal.

**C — True.** Figure 1A places the CCHa1 receptor on the dopamine (DA) neuron, making that neuron the direct neural target of circulating CCHa1. Moreover, the *shi-ts* mutation, which disrupts vesicle endocytosis and exocytosis in the DA neuron, raises arousal from about 27% to about 70% (Figure 1D). Normal output from the DA neuron is therefore needed to suppress arousal. Neurotransmitter secretion requires presynaptic excitation: depolarization and an action potential open voltage-gated calcium channels, and calcium entry triggers synaptic-vesicle fusion. The results consequently support the intended model in which CCHa1 excites the DA neuron and promotes dopamine release, thereby preventing arousal.

**D — False.** TrpA1 conducts Na⁺ and Ca²⁺. Because both ions are less concentrated inside the neuron than outside, opening additional TrpA1 channels drives inward cation current, depolarizes the MBON neuron, and can also enhance transmitter release through Ca²⁺ entry. TrpA1 overexpression lowers the awakened fraction from about 87% to about 31% in Figure 1E, so activating MBON suppresses arousal. The upstream CCHa1 → DA pathway also suppresses arousal and requires DA-neuron transmitter output. In the simple feed-forward circuit shown, dopamine must therefore excite the anti-arousal MBON; an inhibitory DA–MBON synapse would reduce MBON activity and predict the opposite behavioural effect.

**Qualification.** The nutrient supplements were not matched for calories, so Figure 1B alone cannot rigorously exclude every possible non-linear effect of energy intake; it supports the intended nutrient-specific interpretation because expression plainly does not track added calories. Likewise, C and D infer the signs of the two circuit steps from perturbations rather than from direct electrical recordings. These limitations do not change the official truth pattern. The embedded official explanation also refers to “Figures 2–4,” although the relevant data are actually panels B–E of Figure 1.
