# Part B — Task 46

*Osmotic stress, mitochondrial uncoupling, and activity in a marine tardigrade*

**Official answer: A — False; B — True; C — False; D — True.**

## Reasoning

**A — False.** The single-stress treatments do not show chemical stress having a larger effect than osmotic stress. During the first 24 hours, activity falls from about $100\%$ to about $0\%$ both under hypoosmotic conditions (B), under hyperosmotic conditions (C), and with DNP in normal habitat seawater (D). After the animals are returned to normal seawater, all three groups recover to roughly $90$–$100\%$ activity by 72 hours, and the caption states that none differs significantly from the control A at that time. Thus DNP alone affects activity strongly, but no more strongly than either osmotic extreme in these data.

**B — True.** DNP uncouples oxidative phosphorylation, dissipating the mitochondrial proton gradient and thereby greatly reducing ATP production. Hypoosmotic exposure alone in B is reversible: after 24 hours in ultrapure water, the tardigrades recover to about $90$–$100\%$ activity in normal seawater. In E, however, DNP is present during the same hypoosmotic exposure, and activity remains only about $20\%$ even 48 hours after the animals have been returned to normal seawater; its 72-hour value is significantly below that of B. DNP alone in D also permits near-complete recovery. The persistent effect specifically seen in the combined treatment therefore supports the conclusion that ATP-dependent processes—such as restoration of ion and cell-volume balance—are crucial for tolerating and recovering from hypoosmotic stress.

**C — False.** The required comparison was not performed. G applies DNP in normal seawater for 24 hours and then hyperosmotic seawater for 24 hours, so it tests the sequence DNP $\rightarrow$ hyperosmotic stress. There is no corresponding DNP $\rightarrow$ hypoosmotic treatment. E applies DNP and hypoosmotic water simultaneously, while H applies the reverse sequence, hyperosmotic stress $\rightarrow$ DNP. Consequently, the 72-hour results cannot establish that DNP followed by either osmotic extreme has the same effect.

**D — True.** In D, the animals receive DNP while remaining in their normal habitat seawater, so the osmotic condition itself is not stressful. By 24 hours their activity has fallen to zero and the accompanying image shows the contracted morphology, rather than the extended active form in control A. DNP alone is therefore sufficient to trigger the illustrated morphological change; an osmotic shift is not required.

**Data-interpretation qualification.** DNP is an experimental proxy for impaired mitochondrial ATP production, not a selective removal of ATP, and as a protonophore it can have additional physiological effects. Thus B is the intended inference from the treatment comparisons rather than a direct measurement of cellular ATP or of a particular ATP-dependent mechanism. This limitation does not alter the official answer pattern.
