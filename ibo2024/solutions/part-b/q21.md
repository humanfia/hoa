# Part B — Task 21

*Size asymmetry and cannibalism in Japanese mitten crabs*

**Official answer: A — True; B — False; C — False; D — True.**

## Reasoning

**A — True.** In the male-only experiment (Figure 1A), the cannibalism probability is $0.5$ at approximately $S=0.43$. Using the definition of Factor $S$,

\[
0.43=1-\frac{\text{prey size}}{\text{predator size}},
\]

so

\[
\frac{\text{prey size}}{\text{predator size}}=0.57
\quad\text{and}\quad
\frac{\text{predator size}}{\text{prey size}}=\frac{1}{0.57}\approx1.75.
\]

Thus a predator about $1.75$ times the prey's size corresponds to a $50\%$ cannibalism probability among males.

**B — False.** A larger $S$ means that the prey is smaller relative to the predator. The male curve reaches $50\%$ probability at $S\approx0.43$, whereas the female curve reaches it only at $S\approx0.79$. The corresponding predator-to-prey size ratios are about

\[
\frac{1}{1-0.43}\approx1.75
\quad\text{for males, and}\quad
\frac{1}{1-0.79}\approx4.76
\quad\text{for females}.
\]

Females therefore require a much greater size advantage to show the same probability of cannibalism. The graphs indicate a greater tendency to cannibalize in males, not females. The official explanation ends this comparison with a stray “75,” apparently an editing remnant; it does not affect the **False** key.

**C — False.** Figure 1D is explicitly described as hypothetical and shows the number of surviving injured crabs increasing with the number of legs lost. That pattern is not expected in a food-deprived cannibalistic group. A severely injured crab is likely to be attacked again and consumed, so it may be recorded as killed rather than remaining in the sample of injured survivors. This survival bias should underrepresent crabs with the greatest number of lost legs, rather than produce the strong increase shown in Figure 1D. The official explanation's final sentence says that the “level of injury” decreases as the number of lost legs increases; more precisely, it is the **observed frequency among surviving injured crabs** that is expected to decrease as injury severity increases.

**D — True.** Figure 1C gives the killed group a mean $S$ of roughly $0.63$, compared with roughly $0.51$ for the injured group, and marks the difference as significant ($p<0.05$). Because

\[
S=1-\frac{\text{prey size}}{\text{predator size}},
\]

the higher $S$ in killed cases means that the victim was smaller relative to the cannibal. Hence relative size is associated with whether an encounter ends in death or only injury.
