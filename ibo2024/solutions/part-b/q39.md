# Part B — Task 39

*Peripheral arterial disease, intermittent claudication, and cardiovascular drugs*

**Official answer: A — True; B — True; C — False; D — True.**

## Reasoning

**A — True.** The combination of high plasma cholesterol, exercise-induced pain in only one leg, relief with rest, and a reduced pulse pressure in that leg is consistent with atherosclerotic narrowing of a leg artery. A statin lowers atherogenic cholesterol, slowing further plaque growth and helping to stabilize existing plaques. Aspirin inhibits platelet aggregation, so it reduces the chance that a thrombus will form on a disrupted plaque and further obstruct the vessel. These treatments reduce progression and complications, but neither one directly removes an established stenotic plaque and immediately restores the arterial lumen. They therefore do not constitute a complete cure of the existing obstruction.

**B — True.** During walking, the active leg muscles require more oxygen, but the narrowed artery cannot increase blood flow sufficiently. The resulting ischemia increases anaerobic metabolism and the accumulation of acidic metabolites, lowering local pH and activating acid-sensitive receptors. Table 1 gives the peripheral-chemoreceptor threshold as pH below 6.9 both with and without prostaglandins. Blocking prostaglandin synthesis with aspirin therefore does not alter the threshold for the acid stimulus that the question identifies as the cause of this pain. By contrast, prostaglandins do lower the heat-receptor threshold from 46°C to 38°C and the mechanoreceptor threshold from 8 to 3 millinewtons, but those are not the stated trigger for claudication. Aspirin can reduce later thrombotic complications, yet it will not promptly reverse the fixed restriction in blood flow, so it is not an efficient painkiller for these episodes in the model presented.

**C — False.** Systemic hypertension would raise arterial pressure throughout the systemic circulation; it does not explain why the right leg and left arm have normal measurements while only the left leg has a markedly reduced value. This asymmetric loss of pressure points to a local arterial stenosis or obstruction. When exercise raises oxygen demand downstream of that obstruction, oxygen delivery becomes inadequate and the claudication appears; rest lowers demand and relieves it. Thus local peripheral arterial disease, rather than systemic hypertension, is the direct cause supported by the observations.

**D — True.** Appropriate beta-blockers can be used as long-term therapy in stable chronic heart failure. By blocking excessive sympathetic stimulation, they lower heart rate, reduce renin release, limit harmful cardiac remodeling, and reduce the heart's long-term workload and oxygen demand. Their immediate reduction of contractility can be undesirable in acute, decompensated heart failure, so treatment is normally introduced cautiously after the patient is stable. The statement says that beta-1 blockers *might* be used for chronic, long-term treatment, which is therefore correct.

**Physiology qualification.** The question describes beta-1 blockers as systemically relaxing vascular smooth muscle, and the embedded official explanation similarly treats them as vasodilators. That is not a general mechanism of selective beta-1 blockade: beta-1 receptors act principally in the heart and renin-secreting kidney cells, whereas direct beta-mediated vascular relaxation is mainly associated with beta-2 receptors. Some beta-blocking drugs have additional vasodilating actions, but this should not be generalized to the whole class. The official verdict for D remains defensible because selected beta-blockers benefit stable chronic heart failure through cardiac and neurohormonal effects, not because every beta-1 blocker directly relaxes blood vessels.
