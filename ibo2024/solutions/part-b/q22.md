# Part B — Task 22

*Genomic imprinting of the IGF2–H19 cluster and fetal growth*

**Official answer: A — False; B — False; C — True; D — True.**

## Reasoning

**A — False.** The female and male symbols in Figure 1 label the **maternal and paternal chromosome copies**, respectively; they do not represent female and male infants. Every normally developing infant inherits one copy of each parental origin, regardless of the infant's sex. On the maternal copy, the unmethylated imprinting control region directs the enhancer toward active *H19* while *IGF2* is inactive. On the paternal copy, methylation changes the enhancer interaction so that *IGF2* is active while *H19* is inactive. Thus normal *IGF2* and *H19* expression is determined by parental origin, not by whether the infant is male, and the figure provides no basis for a higher cancer risk in male infants.

**B — False.** With paternal uniparental disomy, both chromosome 11 copies have the paternal imprinting pattern. Consequently, the *IGF2* growth-promoting allele can be active on both copies instead of only one, while the normally active maternal *H19* allele is absent. This raises *IGF2* activity and promotes excessive fetal growth, which is in the direction of Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome. Silver–Russell syndrome has the opposite growth-restricted phenotype and would instead be associated with reduced *IGF2* action.

**C — True.** The question states that *IGF2R* is paternally imprinted, so the paternal allele is silent and the maternal allele supplies the functional receptor. A loss-of-function mutation in the maternal allele therefore leaves little or no functional IGF2R. Because IGF2R normally binds IGF2 and sends it to lysosomes for degradation, losing IGF2R increases the amount and persistence of the pro-proliferative IGF2 signal. The resulting fetal overgrowth resembles the macrosomia characteristic of Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome.

**D — True.** Parent-of-origin effects are favoured by conflict between maternal and paternal genetic interests over investment in offspring. Under polygamy, a fetus's paternal alleles are less likely than its maternal alleles to be present in the mother's offspring by other fathers; selection can therefore favour paternal alleles that extract more maternal resources and maternal alleles that restrain that demand. With monogamy, the same parents are likely to produce future siblings, so the interests of their alleles are more closely aligned and this conflict is weaker. Genomic imprinting is therefore expected to evolve or persist at a lower rate in monogamous than in polygamous species.
