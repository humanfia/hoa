# Part B — Task 43

*Hepatic lobule zonation, portal toxins, and bile flow*

**Official answer: A — False; B — True; C — False; D — True.**

## Reasoning

**A — False.** Blood enters a lobule from branches of the portal vein and hepatic artery at the portal triads, passes successively through periportal zone 1, middle zone 2, and perivenous zone 3, and then drains into the central vein. Oxygen is consumed along this path, so zone 1 receives the most oxygenated blood and zone 3 the least. A sustained reduction in oxygen supply therefore primarily threatens zone 3, not zone 2.

**B — True.** An organic toxin absorbed from the intestine reaches the liver in portal venous blood. It consequently enters the lobule at a peripheral portal triad, in zone 1, before the blood flows inward toward the central vein through zones 2 and 3. In Figure 1B, the horizontal coordinate is distance from the central vein, so increasing $X$ means moving outward toward the portal triad. Line P rises with $X$: it represents a high concentration near the portal entry and a progressively lower concentration toward the central vein, as expected when the circulating parent toxin is taken up or metabolized during passage through the lobule.

**C — False.** Complete inability to secrete bile salts corresponds to $X=0$ in Figure 1C. At that point, the rat curve B has a bile-flow intercept of roughly $90\ \mu\mathrm{L\,kg^{-1}\,min^{-1}}$, whereas the rabbit curve A begins at only about $70\ \mu\mathrm{L\,kg^{-1}\,min^{-1}}$. Thus salt-independent bile flow is higher in the rat than in the rabbit; the statement reverses the comparison.

**D — True.** The liver mass is $1.5\ \mathrm{kg}=1500\ \mathrm{g}$. Its total bile-production rate is therefore

$$
1500\ \mathrm{g}\times 17\ \frac{\mu\mathrm{L}}{\mathrm{g\,h}}
=25{,}500\ \mu\mathrm{L/h}
=25.5\ \mathrm{mL/h}.
$$

Forty minutes is $2/3$ of an hour, so the volume produced is

$$
25.5\ \mathrm{mL/h}\times\frac{2}{3}\ \mathrm{h}=17\ \mathrm{mL}.
$$

**Qualification.** B is true under the graph's intended assumptions that the plotted substance is absorbed from the gut, supplied chiefly through portal blood, and progressively removed as blood crosses the lobule. The general phrase “an organic toxic substance” does not by itself guarantee this profile: absorption, hepatic uptake, metabolism, and possible formation of a more toxic metabolite all depend on the particular compound. This limitation does not alter the official answer.
