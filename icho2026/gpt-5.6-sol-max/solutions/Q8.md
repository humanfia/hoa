## Detailed solution

### 8.1

Carbon changes from oxidation state +4 in \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) to +2 in
\(\mathrm{CO}\), so two electrons are required. Balancing oxygen with water
and hydrogen with protons gives

\[
\mathrm{CO_2+2H^+ +2e^-\longrightarrow CO+H_2O}.
\]

Atoms and charge are balanced: each side has one C, two O, two H, and zero
net charge.

### 8.2

Triethanolamine 2 first loses one electron from the amine lone-pair system:

\[
\mathrm{N(CH_2CH_2OH)_3-e^-\longrightarrow
 [N(CH_2CH_2OH)_3]^{\boldsymbol{\cdot}+}}\quad(\mathbf 3).
\]

Loss of an alpha proton from one of its three equivalent arms gives the
neutral alpha-amino radical

\[
\mathrm{(HOCH_2CH_2)_2N-}\dot{\mathrm C}\mathrm{H-CH_2OH}
\quad(\mathbf 4).
\]

A second one-electron oxidation makes the iminium ion; the most useful Lewis
structure has an N=C double bond:

\[
\mathrm{(HOCH_2CH_2)_2N^+=CH-CH_2OH}\quad(\mathbf 5).
\]

Water adds to the iminium carbon. Proton transfer followed by C-N cleavage
then produces

\[
\mathrm{(HOCH_2CH_2)_2NH}\quad(\mathbf 6),\qquad
\mathrm{HOCH_2CHO}\quad(\mathbf 7).
\]

The aldehyde group in glycolaldehyde 7 accounts for the positive Tollens
silver-mirror test. The overall oxidation half-reaction is a useful atom and
charge check:

\[
\mathrm{N(CH_2CH_2OH)_3+H_2O\longrightarrow
 (HOCH_2CH_2)_2NH+HOCH_2CHO+2H^+ +2e^-}.
\]

It contains \(\mathrm{C_6H_{17}NO_4}\) on each side and has zero net charge
on each side.

### 8.3

Ligand 8 supplies four neutral pyridyl-N donors. The two chlorido ligands in
1 add two more donor atoms, so \(\mathrm{CN(Fe)}=6\). The corresponding
geometry is octahedral (distorted by the chelate constraints):

\[
\mathbf 1=\mathrm{[Fe^{II}LCl_2]}.
\]

### 8.4

Use the ionic electron-counting convention. Neutral tetradentate \(L\)
donates \(8e^-\); each aqua or carbonyl ligand donates \(2e^-\); and each
X-type ligand donates \(2e^-\). Iron is a group-8 element, so
\(d^n=8-\mathrm{OS}\).

Hydrolysis of 1 replaces both chlorido ligands without changing the Fe
oxidation state:

\[
\mathrm{[Fe^{II}LCl_2]+2H_2O\longrightarrow
[Fe^{II}L(OH_2)_2]^{2+}+2Cl^-}.
\]

This gives 9 with \(Z=+2\), \(\mathrm{CN}=4+2=6\), and
\(\mathrm{VE}=6+8+2(2)=18\).

One-electron reduction of 9 gives Fe(I), and loss of one water gives 10:

\[
\mathbf{10}=\mathrm{[Fe^IL(OH_2)]^+},\qquad
\mathrm{VE}=7+8+2=17.
\]

The remaining water is replaced by \(\mathrm{CO_2}\). The Fe(I) electron is
transferred into the bound substrate, so the oxidation-state Lewis form of 11
is Fe(II) bonded through carbon to bent \(\mathrm{CO_2^{\boldsymbol{\cdot}-}}\).
That radical-anion fragment is X-type at Fe; its radical remains
ligand-centered. Hence

\[
\mathbf{11}=\mathrm{[Fe^{II}L{-}CO_2^{\boldsymbol{\cdot}-}]^+},\qquad
\mathrm{VE(Fe)}=6+8+2=16.
\]

Addition of one electron and one proton converts that fragment into the
closed-shell, carbon-bound hydroxycarbonyl (\(\mathrm{COOH^-}\)) ligand:

\[
\mathbf{12}=\mathrm{[Fe^{II}L{-}C(=O)OH]^+}.
\]

It is still five-coordinate and 16-electron. Binding water gives the
six-coordinate, 18-electron species

\[
\mathbf{13}=\mathrm{[Fe^{II}L(OH_2){-}C(=O)OH]^+}.
\]

Protonation of the hydroxycarbonyl OH and elimination of water leaves an Fe
carbonyl; the already coordinated aqua ligand remains:

\[
\mathbf{14}=\mathrm{[Fe^{II}L(CO)(OH_2)]^{2+}}.
\]

Both CO and water are neutral two-electron donors, so 14 is
six-coordinate and 18-electron. CO dissociation gives

\[
\mathbf{15}=\mathrm{[Fe^{II}L(OH_2)]^{2+}},
\]

which is five-coordinate and 16-electron. Addition of a second water returns
15 to 9. Summing the substrate-changing steps cancels all catalyst species and
recovers the half-reaction from 8.1:

\[
\mathrm{CO_2+2H^+ +2e^-\longrightarrow CO+H_2O}.
\]

The full set of bookkeeping values is therefore the table in the final-answer
section.

### 8.5

Take \(1.000\ \mathrm{g}\) of the loaded material as a basis. Because
\(\omega_{\rm cat}=0.038\), it contains

\[
m_{\rm cat}=0.0380\ \mathrm{g},\qquad
m_{\mathrm{C_3N_4}}=0.962\ \mathrm{g}.
\]

The number of catalyst molecules is

\[
N(1)=\frac{0.0380\ \mathrm{g}}{557.21\ \mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}}}
      (6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}})
    =4.107\times10^{19}.
\]

The support area on this basis is

\[
\begin{aligned}
A&=(0.962\ \mathrm{g})(17.8\ \mathrm{m^2\ g^{-1}})\\
 &=17.1236\ \mathrm{m^2}
  =1.71236\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{nm^2},
\end{aligned}
\]

where \(1\ \mathrm{m^2}=10^{18}\ \mathrm{nm^2}\). Thus

\[
N_{\rm cat}=\frac{4.107\times10^{19}}
                  {1.71236\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{nm^2}}
            =2.398\ \mathrm{nm^{-2}}
            \simeq \boxed{2.4\ \mathrm{nm^{-2}}}.
\]

### 8.6

For a \(10.0\ \mathrm{mg}=0.0100\ \mathrm{g}\) sample of loaded material,
the amount and number of catalyst molecules are

\[
\begin{aligned}
n_{\rm cat}
 &=\frac{(0.0100\ \mathrm{g})(0.038)}
         {557.21\ \mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}}}
   =6.820\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{mol},\\
N_{\rm cat}
 &=(6.820\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{mol})
   (6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}})
   =4.107\times10^{17}.
\end{aligned}
\]

During one hour, a TOF of \(8\ \mathrm{h^{-1}}\) therefore produces

\[
N_{\rm CO}=8N_{\rm cat}=3.285\times10^{18}
\]

CO molecules. From 8.1, each CO requires two reacted electrons, so

\[
N(e^-)=2N_{\rm CO}=6.571\times10^{18}.
\]

At \(\lambda=390\ \mathrm{nm}\), the energy of one photon is

\[
E_{\rm ph}=\frac{hc}{\lambda}
=\frac{(6.626\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s})
       (2.998\times10^8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})}
      {390\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{m}}
=5.094\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}.
\]

The incident energy in one hour and the corresponding number of photons are

\[
E_{\rm inc}=(0.050\ \mathrm{J\,s^{-1}})(3600\ \mathrm{s})=180\ \mathrm{J},
\]

\[
N_{\rm ph}=\frac{180\ \mathrm{J}}
                  {5.094\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}}
          =3.534\times10^{20}.
\]

Consequently,

\[
\varphi=\frac{6.571\times10^{18}}{3.534\times10^{20}}(100\%)
=1.859\%\simeq\boxed{1.9\%}.
\]

### 8.7

For one gram of loaded material, the number of catalyst molecules is
proportional to \(\omega_{\rm cat}\). Hence the overall CO rate is
proportional to

\[
r_{\rm CO}\mathrel{\propto}\omega_{\rm cat}\,\mathrm{TOF}.
\]

Using the six points read from the graph gives the following proportional
values (the common factor \(N_A/(100M_{\rm cat})\) is omitted):

\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrr}
\omega_{\rm cat}(\%)&0.1&0.3&0.6&1.0&2.0&2.9\\ \hline
\omega_{\rm cat}(\%)\mathrm{TOF}&6.2&16.8&22.2&29&30&31.9
\end{array}
\]

The sequence is monotonically increasing. If the rate is normalized per gram
of support rather than per gram of loaded material, the additional factor
\(1/(1-\omega_{\rm cat})\) is also increasing and does not change the result.
Therefore the correct choice is **a) increases**.

### 8.8

Without irradiation no photochemical products are formed, so the zero-height
entry is \(a=N\). Photon energy follows

\[
E_{\rm photon}(B)>E_{\rm photon}(G)>E_{\rm photon}(R).
\]

The Gibbs-energy diagram shows that proton reduction to \(\mathrm{H_2}\) is
the much more energy-demanding competing route, whereas the CO route remains
accessible at lower photon energy. Thus the fraction of \(\mathrm{H_2}\)
increases in the order red < green < blue. In the bar chart the H2 fractions
increase in the order \(d<c<b\), giving

\[
a=N,\qquad b=B,\qquad c=G,\qquad d=R.
\]

### 8.9

For either excited state, all unquenched first-order decay channels together
have rate constant

\[
k_0=\frac{1}{\tau_0}.
\]

At reductant concentration \([\mathrm{Red}]\), reductive quenching adds the
pseudo-first-order rate \(k_q[\mathrm{Red}]\). The fraction of excited states
that undergo quenching is therefore

\[
\eta_q=\frac{k_q[\mathrm{Red}]}
             {\tau_0^{-1}+k_q[\mathrm{Red}]}
      =\frac{k_q\tau_0[\mathrm{Red}]}
             {1+k_q\tau_0[\mathrm{Red}]}.
\]

For \(S_1\),

\[
k_S\tau_0(S_1)[\mathrm{Red}]
=(2.7\times10^9)(2.9\times10^{-9})(0.1)=0.783,
\]

so

\[
\eta_q(S_1)=\frac{0.783}{1+0.783}(100\%)
=43.91\%\simeq\boxed{44\%}.
\]

For \(T_1\), first convert \(84\ \mathrm{\mu s}=84\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s}\):

\[
k_T\tau_0(T_1)[\mathrm{Red}]
=(1.5\times10^8)(84\times10^{-6})(0.1)=1260,
\]

and hence

\[
\eta_q(T_1)=\frac{1260}{1261}(100\%)
=99.9207\%\simeq\boxed{99.9\%}.
\]

Both results lie between 0 and 100%; the near-quantitative triplet quenching
is consistent with its much longer unquenched lifetime.

### 8.10

The lifetime in the presence of reductant is

\[
\tau=\frac{1}{\tau_0^{-1}+k_q[\mathrm{Red}]}
    =\frac{\tau_0}{1+k_q\tau_0[\mathrm{Red}]}.
\]

Since \(k_q>0\), increasing \([\mathrm{Red}]\) increases the denominator and
therefore decreases the lifetimes of both states. For example, at the
concentration in 8.9,

\[
\tau(S_1)=1.63\ \mathrm{ns},\qquad
\tau(T_1)=6.66\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{s}=66.6\ \mathrm{ns},
\]

both below their respective \(\tau_0\) values. The correct choice is
**b) decreases**.
